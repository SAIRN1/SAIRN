## 1. Vermont — an exclusive territorial franchise, with the incumbents named in the statute

Vermont was in group A. The reason it looked empty is that **two of its three
plausibly-named chapters are decoys**:

- **18 V.S.A. ch. 50, "Home Health Services"** — three sections: § 2201
  *furnishing supervision to home health agencies*, § 2202 *contracts for
  departmental programs*, § 2203 *collection of fees*. **A departmental
  contracting chapter.**
- **18 V.S.A. ch. 57, "Home Care Services"** — §§ 2501–2503 **Recodified**, 1989.
  Empty.

**The real regime is 33 V.S.A. ch. 63, and it is not licensure at all.**

> **§ 6304(a)**: "The designation shall provide each designated agency **a
> franchise** to provide home health services within one or more geographic
> service areas within which it shall have the obligation and responsibility of
> providing home health services **for four years** … **No home health agency
> shall render home health services to patients residing in a designated service
> area without being designated** by the Commissioner to serve patients in that
> service area."

**§ 6304(b) then hard-codes the initial franchises into the statute, by company
name and by town** — Addison County Home Health & Hospice for the towns of
Addison County except Hancock and Granville; VNA & Hospice of Southwestern
Vermont for Pownal, Bennington, Woodford, Shaftsbury and Glastenbury; Franklin
County Home Health Agency for Franklin County; and so on down the list.

**§ 6307** leaves exactly one door: the Commissioner "may enter into agreements
with home health agencies or with any public or private agency **for the purpose
of establishing specialized home health services needed but not available from
the designated home health agencies.**"

> **Alabama at least computes a need number. Vermont wrote the incumbents into
> the code.** This is the strongest market-entry rule found in thirty-one rounds,
> and it is a different mechanism from CON: not "prove need", but **"you are not
> the designated agency."**

**And subchapter 2 is a second worker class on the opposite model.** § 6321
*Attendant care services* establishes a **Participant-Directed** programme, a
**Group-Directed** programme and a personal-services programme, and states that
**"[r]ecipients of attendant care services shall have the opportunity to hire,
train, and terminate the employment of attendants as necessary, establish work
schedules, manage the services, and oversee payments of attendants and
recordkeeping."**

> **Vermont therefore runs a closed franchise for skilled care and a
> consumer-as-employer model for attendant care, in the same state.** That is a
> new axis on its own: **who employs and directs the worker.**

Also worth keeping: **§ 6309** lets an agency deny a subsequent admission, or
decline to send an employee into a home, where a person was previously
discharged to protect staff safety and the risk cannot be mitigated — with an
explicit statement that nothing requires the agency to enter the home to find
out.

## 2. New Hampshire — two licence categories, a hard bar, and a waiver

NH has **two separate licensing Parts**, which is the split stated as plainly as
any state states it:

- **He-P 809 — Home Health Care Providers (HHCP)**
- **He-P 822 — Home Care Service Provider Agencies (HCSPA)**

**He-P 822.17 (Personnel)** is agency-defined on qualifications and hard-edged on
disqualification:

- The licensee writes a **job description per position** carrying duties,
  physical requirements and "qualifications and educational requirements" — the
  Arizona pattern.
- For **all applicants, all volunteers and all independent contractors** who
  provide direct or personal care: **criminal record check** (except Board of
  Nursing licensees, per RSA 151:2-d, VI), **BEAS elder-and-adult registry
  check**, and verification of qualifications before employment.
- **Categorical bars**: any felony; conviction for sexual assault, other violent
  crime, assault, fraud, theft, abuse, neglect or exploitation; **an
  administrative finding** of the same in any state; or "otherwise poses a threat
  to the health, safety, or well-being of clients."
- **A waiver, and it is mandatory in form**: "The department **shall** grant a
  waiver … if, after reviewing the underlying circumstances, it determines that
  the person does not pose a threat." **Second explicit second-chance mechanism
  found**, after Alaska's 7 AAC 10.935.
- **A continuing duty**: if the disqualifying fact surfaces after hire, the
  licensee must notify the department and either stop using the person or request
  a waiver.
- **§ (h) permits delegated vetting**: in lieu of doing the checks itself, the
  licensee "may accept from independent agencies contracted by the licensee …
  **a signed statement that the agency's employees have complied**."
- Baseline for all personnel: orientation before any client contact covering
  seven named topics **including body mechanics**; mandatory annual in-service on
  four; **minimum age 18 for direct care personnel** unless an LNA under RN
  supervision or in an established educational programme; and a physical or
  health screening plus **2-step Mantoux TB testing within the prior 12 months**,
  with permission to work between the first and second step if the first is
  negative.

## 3. Kentucky — training triggered by the client's diagnosis

**902 KAR 20:081**, amended to implement **KRS 216.9375** (SB 61, 2021):

> A home health aide "who provides care to a patient that **exhibits symptoms of
> Alzheimer's disease or other dementia** shall complete at least **six (6) hours
> of initial training and three (3) hours of annual training** in dementia care."
>
> The initial training must be completed **before** providing services to such a
> patient.
>
> And: an aide "who has a **lapse of twenty-four (24) months or more** providing
> care to a patient that exhibits symptoms of Alzheimer's disease or other
> dementia shall complete the six (6) hours of initial training **within sixty
> (60) days of resuming**."

> **This is a training obligation keyed to the client's condition, with its own
> recency clock.** Connecticut's 24-month lapse rule voids the whole credential;
> **Kentucky's is scoped to one diagnosis** — an aide can be fully current
> generally and disqualified for dementia clients specifically. **A single
> "trained: yes/no" field cannot hold this, and neither can a single expiry
> date.**

*(The same amendment let advanced practice registered nurses and physician
assistants supervise home health services in lieu of physicians — a
supervisor-class widening worth noting against states that still say
"physician".)*

## 4. Maine — "direct access personnel", and the chore-services carve-out

**Round 15's Maine citation was wrong** — `title22ch1666` is *Appointment of
Receivers*. **The chapter is 22 M.R.S. ch. 419, Home Health Services.**
*(Pattern-log incident #2 is now resolved; see
`sairnsenior-PATTERN-LOG-wrong-citations.md`.)*

Maine's title also carries **ch. 1622**, **ch. 1623** (in-home and community
support services for the elderly and other adults) and **ch. 1625** — the last
now **fully repealed**.

**§ 2147 exclusions** are unusually broad and land squarely on the product:

> **families, friends and neighbors** · **sole practitioners otherwise licensed
> by the State** · hospice organisations staffed substantially by volunteers ·
> services provided directly by physicians · elderly nutrition programmes ·
> **chore services** · pharmacies/medical supply companies furnishing only
> supplies · persons or agencies contracting or arranging home health services

> **Minnesota and Maine treat chores in opposite directions.** Minnesota's basic
> licence *covers* housekeeping and shopping **but only as an adjunct** to
> hands-on care (round 30). **Maine excludes chore services from licensure
> altogether.** Same activity, opposite regulatory consequence.

**§ 2149-A** frames the vetting duty around **"direct access personnel"** (defined
at § 1717(1)(A-2)): a comprehensive background check and the employment
restrictions of § 1812-G apply "when hiring, employing or placing direct access
personnel, **including a certified nursing assistant or a direct care worker**."

> **The discriminator is access to the client**, not credential and not task —
> a close cousin of North Carolina's hands-on test, drawn one step wider.

**§ 2149-B, "Home health aide services to minors", is marked
`EFFECTIVE ON CONTINGENCY`.** **Second instance of the not-yet-in-force legal
state**, after South Carolina's dormant licensure (round 30).

## 5. Montana — the non-medical tier is a Medicaid programme, not a licence

**MCA Title 50 ch. 5** licenses health care facilities. **"Home health agency"**
is the only in-home category; **"personal care"** is defined but only as *"the
provision of services and care for **residents**"* — a residential-facility term.

**Montana's non-medical in-home tier sits in Medicaid**: MCA Title 53 ch. 6
**Part 4, "Home and Community-Based Long-Term Care Medicaid Services."**
**Route located, not read.** *(Same shape as Idaho, whose personal-assistance
regime is a Medicaid chapter — round 28.)*

**MCA 50-5, Part 7 "Lay Caregivers" is not a worker class.** It is a CARE-Act
style regime: designation of a **lay caregiver** by a patient, notice to that
person, and **instruction to them** before discharge, with immunity. **An unpaid
designated family caregiver with a statutory right to be trained** — a category
no other state in this survey has surfaced, and not a licensure axis.

## 6. Wyoming — one category, and a definition that reaches arrangers

Wyoming's health care facility definitions carry **"home health agency"** and no
separate non-medical in-home licence. **Homemaker, personal care and chore
services appear only in the adult-protective-services provisions**, as emergency
services the state may arrange — not as a licensed category.

**The definition is short and wide:**

> "**'Home health agency' means an agency primarily engaged in arranging and
> directly providing** nursing or other health care services to persons at their
> residence."

> **"Arranging"** sits inside the licensed activity, **with no
> financial-interest qualifier** — broader on its face than South Carolina's
> referral clause, which at least requires the referrer to have a financial
> interest (round 30). See §8.

## 8. HELD FOR MICHAEL — three states now bear directly on the marketplace model

**Not decided here.** This is a judgment call on par with the Hawai'i
weekend-shift question, and it belongs with
`2026-08-30-sairnsenior-DECISION-GATE-INPUT-marketplace-model.md`.

Three findings from rounds 30–31 point at the same question from different
angles:

1. **South Carolina** (round 30) defines an "in-home care provider" to include a
   business that makes provision for services **"through referral of other
   persons … when the individual making the referral has a financial interest in
   the delivery of those services."** A referral business with a financial
   interest **is** the licensed thing.
2. **Wyoming** puts **"arranging"** inside the definition of a home health agency
   **with no financial-interest qualifier at all.**
3. **Vermont** forbids any home health agency from serving patients in a
   designated service area **without being the designated agency**, and names the
   incumbents in the statute.

**The question, stated neutrally:** does a matching-and-payments platform that
takes a fee become, in these states, the licensed entity itself — a *provider*
rather than an intermediary — and in Vermont, is the skilled tier closed to a new
entrant regardless?

**What is established:** the three provisions, as quoted, from official sources.
**What is not:** how each state actually applies them to a platform. **No state
guidance, no enforcement history and no counsel opinion has been read.** Nevada's
"employer of record" rule and Rhode Island's reclassification clause (the
reclassification sweep) are the nearest prior instances and were treated the same
way.

**Recommended next step, for Michael to accept or reject:** put these three in
front of the decision gate as *scope* questions before any further state reading,
because they bear on whether the marketplace model is available at all in some
states — which is upstream of what the qualification rules say.

## 11. Method notes

- **Two more chapter-name traps, both in Vermont.** "Home Health Services" was
  departmental contracting; "Home Care Services" was recodified in 1989. **The
  count of chapters whose name promised licensure and delivered something else is
  now six** — South Dakota 34-3A, West Virginia art. 2C, North Dakota 23-17.5,
  Vermont 18 ch. 50, Vermont 18 ch. 57, and Maine's mis-cited ch. 1666.
- **"Not in force yet" keeps appearing.** South Carolina's contingent licensure
  and Maine's `EFFECTIVE ON CONTINGENCY` section are the same shape. Two
  instances is a category, not an oddity.
- **A repealed chapter still returns clean text** — Maine ch. 1625's three
  sections all render, each marked `(REPEALED)`. Read the marker, not just the
  section list.
- **New Jersey is recorded as unresolved, not blocked.** Every state this session
  that was called blocked turned out not to be.
